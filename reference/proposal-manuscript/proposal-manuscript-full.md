1. Introduction and Problem Statement 
While the Philippines boasts a high national basic literacy rate, recently recorded at approximately 90% for individuals aged five and above (Philippine Statistics Authority, 2024); this figure masks a deeper, more systemic crisis. The 2024 Functional Literacy, Education, and Mass Media Survey (FLEMMS) reveals a significant decrease when moving from basic to functional literacy. For every ten Filipinos, nine can read and write, but only seven (approximately 70.8%) possess the high-level comprehension skills necessary to integrate information, make inferences, and apply knowledge in complex, real-world scenarios (PSA, 2024). This literacy gap translates to around 19 million Filipinos (aged 10-64) who are “basic literate” but “functionally illiterate” [CITATION NEEDED: source or derivation of the 19 million figure]. In an era where the global labor market is being aggressively reshaped by Artificial Intelligence and digital-first workflows, this deficit creates a "labor force lag" that threatens to stall the Philippines’ trajectory toward high-income status.
Historically, policy interventions by agencies such as DepEd and DOLE have been hampered by a reliance on lagging, aggregate national indicators that mask severe localized disparities. However, the landmark release of provincial and Highly Urbanized City (HUC) level data in the 2024 FLEMMS provides an unprecedented opportunity to map the "spatial architecture" of illiteracy. Beyond geography, the divide is reinforced by numerous factors. First, access to the internet and digital tools is no longer a luxury but a prerequisite for functional literacy. The "digital divide" between urban centers like San Juan City (94.5% functional literacy [CITATION NEEDED]) and remote provinces creates a cycle where those without connectivity cannot practice the digital comprehension skills required by the modern labor market. And lastly, Intergenerational Literacy. Recent studies indicate that children of functionally illiterate parents perform below expected reading levels (Biñas & Garvida, 2023), suggesting that illiteracy is a self-perpetuating cycle if not interrupted by targeted state intervention. This granular perspective reveals that functional literacy is not merely an educational outcome, bounded within the school premises, but a product of a complex socio-technical ecosystem, one driven by the digital divide, intergenerational cycles of learning poverty, and regional economic shifts.
The current reactive paradigm of resource allocation is no longer sufficient to bridge these divides. Existing interventions are often broad-stroke and lack the precision to account for localized drivers such as socio-economic shifts, linguistic diversity, or infrastructure deficits. Consequently, there is an urgent need for a transition toward anticipatory governance. By leveraging the 2024 FLEMMS dataset, this thesis proposes the development of a Predictive Early Warning System (EWS). This system is designed to identify emerging "hotspots" of functional illiteracy before they manifest in systemic unemployment or economic stagnation. By transforming static survey data into a dynamic, predictive tool, this research aims to provide policymakers with the precision required to deploy targeted, data-driven interventions, thereby securing the nation’s human capital and ensuring equitable development across the archipelago.
1.1. Research Objectives
To address these disparities, the primary goal of this research is to transition from traditional descriptive reporting to a proactive, predictive framework. Specifically, the study is guided by the following objectives:
To describe, as a precursor to prediction, where functional literacy risk is geographically concentrated among working-age Filipinos across provinces and Highly Urbanized Cities (HUCs), using Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA).
To identify and quantify the socioeconomic, demographic, and spatial factors (e.g., internet access, income dynamics, and infrastructure) that are associated with functional illiteracy risk.
To develop a predictive Early Warning System (EWS) using Gradient Boosting models, and to use it to simulate policy scenarios and target interventions for high-risk areas.
1.2. Significance and Motivation
The motivation for this study stems from the observed inefficiencies in current resource allocation for educational and labor interventions. Because functional literacy impacts the broader working-age population from ages 10 to 64, addressing these disparities is critical for both educational development and labor force competency.
This research is significant as it provides a decision-support framework for evidence-based policy and localized governance. By demonstrating how data can create Provincial and HUC-level risk profiles, the study enables national agencies like DepEd and DOLE to shift from generalized regional modules to targeted local interventions. Furthermore, by identifying specific root causes such as digital competency gaps or physical health barriers, the research facilitates inter-agency coordination, allowing departments to synchronize local programs specifically where the model predicts the highest risk. Ultimately, this data-driven prioritization ensures that limited public funds, such as the Local School Board (LSB) and Special Education Funds (SEF), are directed toward the areas with the greatest learning poverty needs.
1.3. Scope and Limitations
The scope of this study focuses on the Philippine population aged 10 to 64 years old, consistent with the target respondents of the Philippine Statistics Authority’s (PSA) Functional Literacy, Education, and Mass Media Survey (FLEMMS) Form 2 (Literacy) and Form 3 (Individual) questionnaires. The analysis utilizes individual-level records from FLEMMS, supplemented by relevant household-level information available within the survey. The study draws on a large sample of approximately 496,770 observations, with survey weights applied to ensure national representativeness of the findings.
However, the study is subject to several limitations inherent in the use of secondary survey data. Notably, FLEMMS does not include direct measures or predictors of household income or income classification. As a result, the study employs proxy indicators derived from household characteristics, such as housing construction materials and ownership of appliances, as approximations of socioeconomic status. While these proxies provide useful insights, they may not fully capture the complexity and variability of income levels across households.
Additionally, the reliance on localized proxy variables, including housing materials, limits the precision with which physical and economic vulnerability to climate-related risks can be assessed. Finally, although advanced analytical methods such as the XGBoost model and spatial clustering techniques like Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA) offer strong predictive and exploratory capabilities, the results remain constrained by the variables available within the FLEMMS dataset and do not account for external or unobserved qualitative factors that may influence the outcomes. Because FLEMMS is a single cross-section, the EWS predicts current functional literacy status rather than future change, and its scenario simulations describe model associations, not causal effects.

2. Literature Review
2.1 Functional Literacy
Functional literacy refers to the capability of a person to perform in activities, in which literacy is required, as a functioning member of society (UNESCO, 2020). Across all literature definitions, a person’s Functional Literacy level is gauged based on their ability to utilize reading and writing as well as performing calculations essential for daily activities. Historically, however, Philippine literacy definition has operated under an assumption of "automatic qualification" where secondary education completion served as a definitive proxy for functional competency. While prior research was structured around the prevailing standard of 'automatic qualification' via a high school diploma, this study implements the post-2024 benchmarks. This shift acknowledges the distinction between credential acquisition and the higher-level functional skills required by the current national definition.

This difference between credentials and actual competence is fundamentally shaped by geographic context, as provinces differ significantly in their socioeconomic, infrastructure, and demographic compositions. Owada et al. (2019), established the presence of spatial heterogeneity in illiteracy in the Philippines through an ecological study utilizing data from the 2008 Functional Literacy, Education and Mass Media Survey (FLEMMS). However, their spatial analysis was limited in scope to school-aged children. Because the FLEMMS dataset captures demographics from ages 10 to 64, a critical research gap exists: the spatial inequality of literacy must be explored across the broader, working-age population to understand its impact on labor force readiness.  
2.2 Spatial Econometrics and Regional Disparity 
The presence of spatial clustering in educational outcomes is supported by broader spatial econometric literature. While the seminal work of Owada et al. (2019) established visual evidence of spatial heterogeneity through georeferenced mapping and descriptive statistics, their methodology primarily focused on the visualization of geographical variations. There remains a methodological gap in the application of rigorous, inferential spatial econometrics to the Philippine functional literacy landscape.
To address this, techniques such as Moran’s I and Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA), introduced by Anselin (1995), provide a more robust mathematical framework to identify "hot spots" and "cold spots" of development. These methods consistently demonstrate that low educational attainment is geographically entrenched rather than randomly distributed, moving beyond simple visualization to statistically validate spatial clusters. International literature further suggests that this gap is not a constant; for instance, Xiang and Stillwell (2023) highlight that regional economic factors and infrastructure access significantly dictate educational performance. Consequently, by mathematically identifying these "pockets" of disadvantage, interventions can be more effectively coordinated at the regional level rather than relying on generalized national strategies.
2.3 Digital Skills and Resources
Going beyond spatial inequality, more recent literature emphasizes socioeconomic and technological factors as driving forces in shaping functional literacy. Access to digital resources, particularly internet activity, has emerged as a significant determinant to learning outcomes. The World Bank emphasizes that digital inclusion plays a critical role in improving literacy and educational attainment, as well as boosting employment outcomes later on, especially in developing economies where educational resources are often limited (Sabarwal & Noonan, 2025). In the Philippine context, the gaps in access to media and information technology, as captured by FLEMMS, provide a crucial dimension for modeling and understanding functional literacy gaps.
Building upon the role of digital inclusion, scholars argue that the "digital divide" in the Philippines has evolved from a matter of physical infrastructure to a divide in digital agency and cognitive processing. While mobile penetration in the Philippines remains high, the 2024–2025 reports from the Department of Information and Communications Technology (DICT) suggest that access is often restricted to "walled garden" social media environments rather than open educational resources [CITATION NEEDED: DICT report]. This creates a paradox where individuals are digitally connected yet remain "functionally illiterate" in the context of critical information evaluation and complex data synthesis.
Furthermore, the intersectionality of poverty and technology remains a persistent hurdle. Recent longitudinal studies [CITATION NEEDED] indicate that the "app-based economy" in urban centers like Metro Manila and Cebu has incentivized a specific type of functional literacy, one geared toward gig-work navigation–while rural areas continue to struggle with "bandwidth poverty." This geographical disparity in internet quality directly correlates with FLEMMS indicators on basic versus functional literacy; those in regions with poor connectivity score lower in numeracy and long-form comprehension, as their digital interactions are limited to low-data, text-based communication.
Finally, the 2025 World Bank updates on the Philippines underscore that "technological leapfrogging" is only effective when paired with localized content. Functional literacy gaps are narrowed most significantly when digital resources are delivered in mother-tongue languages through offline-first digital platforms [CITATION NEEDED: World Bank 2025 source], suggesting that the future of literacy modeling must account for linguistic accessibility alongside hardware availability.
2.4 Machine Learning as Early Warning System in Education
Another significant limitation in existing Philippine literature is the reliance on descriptive reporting, or rather mapping where literacy gaps currently exist based on historical surveys. In recent years, there has been a growing shift in literature towards the use of predictive analytics and machine learning in improving education policy. Early Warning Systems have been developed and employed to identify and address students at risk of dropping-out or receiving poor educational outcomes. This has enabled for more targeted and proactive intervention in education. These systems typically employ supervised classification models to predict educational outcomes of students. Most literature, such as that presented by Chung and Lee (Chung & Lee, 2019), have found that machine learning approaches (Random Forest, and other Gradient-Boosted algorithms) are better suited to handling high-dimensionality of data and complex non-linear relationships. This allows policymakers to shift from a "post-mortem" analysis of literacy failures to an Early Warning System capable of simulating policy scenarios and targeting interventions before academic and functional deficits become entrenched in the working-age population.
Despite the predictive power of Gradient Boosting models, a persistent gap in the literature is that most XGBoost implementations are 'aspatial’, treating observations as independent and identically distributed regardless of their geographic origin. This approach often fails to account for spatial non-stationarity, the phenomenon where the relationship between independent and dependent variables varies significantly across a geographic landscape (Brunsdon et al., 1996). For example, 'Internet Access' may be a baseline requirement in highly urbanized cities (HUCs) but act as a high-impact differentiator for functional literacy in rural, 'bandwidth-poor' regions. Traditional ML often averages these regional nuances into a single global coefficient, potentially leading to 'geographic bias' in policy recommendations. This study addresses this by applying spatial analytics (LISA) to the XGBoost model’s provincial risk predictions, identifying where predicted risk is geographically clustered. Spatial variation in the determinants themselves is not modeled here and is noted as a limitation.

However, exact Shapley computation grows exponentially with the number of features. TreeSHAP reduces this cost for tree ensembles, but pairwise SHAP interaction values remain expensive on large samples, so we compute them on a test-set subsample. 
Aside from the dangers of computational intensive calculation of SHAP values, relying solely on SHAP presents specific limitations in the context of socio-economic policy making. To establish a robust “Early Warning System” for functional literacy to be used primarily by policymakers and educators, the literature suggests inclusion of other Explainable AI (XAI) techniques. A foundational method in this domain is usage of Partial Dependence Plot (PDP) which “shows the marginal effect one or two features have on the predicted outcome of a machine learning model” (Molnar, 2022). However, PDP assumes that the predictors are independent and have no correlation with one another. In the FLEMMS context, where variables such as “Urbanization” and “Internet Access”, for instance, are intrinsically linked, PDPs may produce biased results by averaging over unlikely data combinations. Instead, Accumulated Local Effects (ALE) serves as a more reliable alternative. Unlike PDP, ALE solves for the change in predictions based on the conditional distribution of features, which effectively isolates the effect of a specific variable without the possible influence of correlated predictors. Unlike PDP, which uses the marginal distribution , ALE uses the conditional distribution , ensuring we only average over realistic data instances. 

The closest familiar analogue is the marginal effect in a linear regression, read ceteris paribus. A regression coefficient is a marginal effect that is constant by construction, so the effect of one more unit of a predictor is the same at every value of that predictor. ALE keeps the idea of an effect of one variable on the outcome but drops the constant-slope assumption. It estimates a local, non-parametric marginal effect: within narrow intervals of a feature, it averages the change in predicted risk over the observations that actually lie in that interval, then accumulates these local changes across the feature’s range. The resulting curve can bend, flatten, or reverse, which lets a policymaker read where along a feature (for example, from no internet use to regular use) predicted illiteracy risk changes most. Because each local change is computed only from realistic neighbours, correlated predictors such as “Urbanization” and “Internet Access” do not contaminate each other’s curves, which a ceteris paribus reading of a regression coefficient or a PDP cannot guarantee when predictors move together.
This provides policymakers with a clearer and unbiased view of how specific factors such as internet access directly impact functional literacy risk.

By employing Feature Interaction measures, such as Friedman’s H-statistic, the research can quantify the degree to which the model's prediction is the result of combined effects (e.g., the intersection of inability to attend schooling and poor digital infrastructure). As noted in the literature, if a model exhibits high interaction effects, explaining the model through individual feature importance alone is insufficient. Integrating these interaction metrics ensures that the "Early Warning System" captures the nuanced, multi-dimensional nature of functional illiteracy across different Philippine regions.

The H-statistic also determines how we report results. If H is close to 1 for a feature pair, single-feature SHAP and ALE rankings no longer describe that pair’s contribution, so we will report two-dimensional ALE or risk surfaces for the pair (for example, predicted risk over an internet access by schooling grid) and frame policy recommendations around the combination rather than either variable alone. If H stays low across pairs, we will report that an additive, single-feature reading is adequate and use the simpler per-feature policy narrative. 2.6 Dimensionality Reduction in Large-Scale Surveys
The FLEMMS dataset is characterized by high dimensionality, containing hundreds of variables ranging from individual demographics to specific household amenities. A significant challenge in utilizing such datasets is multicollinearity, where indicators (e.g., "ownership of a radio" and "ownership of a television") are highly correlated, potentially inflating the variance of model coefficients.
While there is limited literature using PCA specifically on the FLEMMS dataset, researchers in developing economies have historically used PCA to construct Synthetic Socioeconomic Indices. For instance, researchers often follow the Filmer and Pritchett (2001) methodology, which established PCA as the standard for creating "Wealth Indices" from asset-based survey data (like those found in FLEMMS). In this study, we use PCA as an unsupervised diagnostic of redundancy and latent structure in the survey space. Correlated indicators consolidate into components that describe broader respondent profiles, such as access to information or material vulnerability. PCA ranks features by the variance they explain, which says nothing about their relevance to functional literacy. We therefore select the modeling features with a supervised procedure (Section 3.3) and report how much the two selections overlap.
3. Methodology (Research Design)
This study employs a quantitative, predictive, and spatial analytical design. It utilizes secondary data from the 2024 Functional Literacy, Education, and Mass Media Survey (FLEMMS) to develop a Machine Learning (ML) based Early Warning System. The research is divided into two primary phases: (1) Diagnostic Dimensionality Analysis and Supervised Feature Selection, and (2) Predictive Modeling and Interpretability Analysis.
3.1 Data Source and Sample Selection
The primary data source is the 2024 FLEMMS conducted by the Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA). The dataset consists of consolidated individual-level records from four survey volumes: RTF1, RTF2, RTF3, and MEMBER.
Target Population: Filipinos aged 10 to 64 years.
Sampling Design: The study utilizes the 2023 Geo-Enabled Master Sample (GeoMS) framework developed by the PSA for national household-based surveys. This design employs a stratified two-stage cluster sampling approach. 
First Stage: Primary Sampling Units (PSUs), consisting of barangays or groups of adjacent small barangays, are selected with probability proportional to size (PPS).
Second Stage: Secondary Sampling Units (SSUs), which are individual households, are systematically selected from within each PSU.
National Scale: The resulting dataset for this study encompasses a nationally representative sample of approximately 496,770 individual observations. This scale is sufficient to generate reliable estimates at the national, regional, provincial, and highly urbanized city (HUC) levels.(Philippine Statistics Authority, 2024)
Unit of Analysis: Individual survey respondents.
3.2 Data Preprocessing and Cleaning
Data from all volumes were merged into a unified dataset. Geographic identifiers (Region, Province, Municipality) were resolved to prevent redundancy.
Handling of Missing Data: We apply two imputation rules, one per column type. (a) Categorical columns: missing values are imputed as 'Not Applicable' rather than excluded. This is statistically justified by the survey's skip-logic structure; for example, respondents who have not used the internet are naturally excluded from specific digital skill questions, making the 'Missing' status a structural attribute of the respondent's profile rather than a random data loss. (b) Numeric columns (in practice only AGE, since all other columns are treated as categorical): missing values are imputed with the column median, which is robust to outliers and affects few records. The 44 features selected by gain importance in Phase 1 serve as the input for an XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosting) classifier. XGBoost was selected due to its superior handling of tabular data, its ability to capture complex non-linear interactions through gradient-weighted decision trees, and its robust regularization parameters which prevent overfitting in high-dimensional survey data.
Unlike traditional bagging methods (e.g., Random Forest), XGBoost follows a sequential boosting strategy where each subsequent tree is trained to predict the residuals (errors) of the previous ensemble, effectively minimizing a global loss function.

3.4.1 Implementation Details
To transition from the theoretical objective function to a functional Early Warning System (EWS), the XGBoost model will be implemented using the following data science workflow:
Handling Class Imbalance: Since the target variable (Functional Illiteracy) represents a minority class (approximately 30% based on current PSA data) the model will utilize the scale_pos_weight hyperparameter. This ensures that the gradient descent process places a higher penalty on misclassifying illiterate individuals, preventing the model from becoming biased toward the majority "literate" class.
Integration of Survey Weights: A critical component of this implementation is the use of PSA survey weights (RESP_RFACT_F2). These weights will be passed into the training function so that each observation’s contribution to the loss function L(t) is scaled by its representative power in the national population. This ensures that the EWS risk scores reflect the actual scale of the Philippine population rather than just the raw sample count.
Hyperparameter Tuning: The model will be optimized using a randomized hyperparameter search with 5-fold cross-validation. Key parameters for tuning include:
max_depth: To control tree complexity and prevent overfitting to survey-specific noise.
learning_rate (η): To control the step size at each boosting iteration.
gamma (γ): To specify the minimum loss reduction required to make a further partition on a leaf node of the tree.
Performance Metrics: While accuracy is a baseline, the primary evaluation metrics for this EWS will be Precision-Recall (PR) AUC and the F1-Score. These are chosen because they provide a more reliable measure of model performance when dealing with imbalanced socio-economic datasets where the cost of a "False Negative" (failing to identify a high-risk area) is high.
Early Stopping: To ensure the regularization term Ω(f) is effective, an early stopping criterion will be implemented. If the log-loss on a withheld validation set does not improve for 50 consecutive iterations, the training will terminate to preserve the model's generalizability.
3.5 Model Interpretability and Explainability
Beyond predictive accuracy, the model emphasizes explainability for policy intervention:
Global Interpretability: Feature importance rankings from XGBoost (gain).
Local and Magnitude Impact: SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) values to determine the direction and strength of each feature’s influence on literacy risk.
Advanced XAI: Accumulated Local Effects (ALE) curves show how predicted illiteracy risk shifts as a feature moves across its range, using only realistic data regions (Section 2.5). 3.6 Spatial Diagnostics and Scenario Simulation (Proposed)
The integration of spatial analytics with machine learning is implemented as a post-predictive diagnostic layer. It involves aggregating individual-level risk predictions at the provincial level. While the XGBoost model uses the 44 selected original features to generate globalized risk probabilities, Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA) are subsequently applied to the model's outputs. First, LISA will be used to assess whether provinces with high (or low) survey-weighted mean predicted illiteracy risk are geographically concentrated rather than randomly distributed. This will allow classification of provinces into four categories: High-High clusters (high-risk provinces surrounded by similarly high-risk provinces), Low-Low clusters (low-risk provinces surrounded by similarly low-risk provinces), and spatial outliers (High-Low and Low-High).
